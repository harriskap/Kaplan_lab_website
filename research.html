Research

Understanding the developing brain

We combine single-cell genomics with in vivo neural recordings and genetic manipulations in mice to ask how infant-specific circuits are built, how they drive behavior, and how behavior shapes the brain in return.

"Developing animals behave differently from adults. How do these age-specific behaviors arise from the brain — and how do they feed back to influence brain development itself?"

The hypothalamus and brainstem are ancient brain regions that coordinate the body's most fundamental needs: temperature, sleep, hunger, and social bonds. These regions contain hundreds of genetically distinct neuron types, yet the functions of most remain completely unknown. Our lab sits at the intersection of two questions: What do all these cell types do? And: how do early-life experiences write themselves into the molecular identity of these neurons? We use the neonatal mouse as our primary model, because the first weeks of life represent a critical window in which circuits are being assembled and tested against the demands of a developing body.

01
Research Focus

Early-Life Behaviors

A newborn mouse pup spends roughly 80% of its time in sleep, cannot regulate its own body temperature, and relies entirely on its mother and littermates for warmth and nutrition. These behaviors are mediated by circuits that are distinct from — and often more constrained than — those that serve the same functions in adults.

We want to understand the neural substrates of infant-specific behaviors: the hypothalamic and brainstem circuits that control neonatal sleep, the ultrasonic vocalizations that recruit parental care, and the sensory and social interactions between pups, parents, and siblings. By combining behavioral assays with targeted neural recordings and manipulations in neonatal mice, we map the circuits that are active during these behaviors and ask what happens to behavior when those circuits are altered.

Key Questions

  • Which hypothalamic populations orchestrate neonatal sleep and arousal?
  • How do pup ultrasonic vocalizations and maternal responses form a feedback circuit?
  • How do infant circuits change across postnatal development into adulthood?
  • What sensory signals trigger parental behavior, and which neurons detect them?

02
Research Focus

Hypothalamic Neuronal Diversity

Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed that the hypothalamus contains hundreds of molecularly distinct neuron types, each defined by a unique combination of transcription factors, neuropeptides, and receptors. Despite this remarkable diversity, the behavioral functions of the vast majority of these cell types remain completely unknown — they are "orphaned" from any known function.

We take a high-throughput "de-orphaning" approach: systematically activating or silencing molecularly defined populations using AAV-based tools and asking what behaviors change. Combined with recordings of neural activity during naturalistic behavior, this allows us to build a functional map of the subcortical circuits that control homeostasis, social behavior, and early-life physiology. Our long-term goal is to understand the organizing principles that relate molecular cell-type identity to neural circuit function.

Key Questions

  • What behaviors are controlled by each hypothalamic cell type?
  • How does molecular identity relate to a neuron's inputs, outputs, and activity?
  • Are there organizing principles linking cell-type identity to circuit function?
  • How do these cell types interact during complex, multi-component behaviors?

03
Research Focus

Experience and Brain Development

The principle that activity shapes circuit development is well-established in sensory systems — but it has been much less explored in subcortical regions like the hypothalamus. Our work suggests this principle applies broadly. In a recent study published in Nature (2025), we showed that sensory input during early postnatal life, together with biological sex and ongoing neuronal activity, shapes the molecular identity of cell types in the hypothalamic preoptic area.

This raises a new set of questions: Which infant behaviors generate the relevant sensory signals? Are there critical or sensitive periods for hypothalamic development? And how does early-life experience affect the function of adult circuits? We address these questions using mouse genetics, behavioral manipulations, and longitudinal recordings across development.

Key Questions

  • Do infant behaviors generate the sensory signals that shape hypothalamic development?
  • Are there critical periods for hypothalamic cell-type specification?
  • How does early-life sensory experience affect adult circuit function?
  • What molecular mechanisms link activity to cell-type identity changes?

Methods

Our Approach

We bring together molecular and systems neuroscience tools to connect genetically defined cell types to circuit function and behavior.

🧬

Single-Cell Genomics

Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics to map cell-type diversity across the hypothalamus and brainstem, and to track how molecular identities change across development and with experience.

🔬

In Vivo Calcium Imaging

Fiber photometry and miniscope-based imaging to record activity of genetically defined populations in freely behaving neonatal and adult mice during natural behaviors.

💡

Circuit Manipulation

Optogenetics and chemogenetics (DREADDs) delivered via AAV vectors to activate or silence specific neuron types and ask what behaviors change. Enables systematic "de-orphaning" of hypothalamic cell types.

🐭

Behavioral Analysis

Custom behavioral assays for neonatal and adult mice, including automated sleep scoring, thermal preference, ultrasonic vocalization analysis, parent–pup interaction paradigms, and high-resolution video tracking.
